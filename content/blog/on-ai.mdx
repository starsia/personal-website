I first heard about the wonders of AI (back then led by ChatGPT) in January of 2023, nearing my second year of university. My peers in Computer Science were talking non-stop about a tool that could solve algorithm problems and our challenging OOP assignments in a flash. I didn't believe them, and I stubbornly did not want to give up on my question set.

## The test

A few days later, stuck on an algorithms question myself for over an hour, I gave it a try. It gave me an answer that was more a scaffold of what I needed to start. I was amazed. How? I will not go into the technicalities that underpin an LLM. Concepts like BERT, embeddings, stemming, tokens and weights are something that I gradually wrapped my head around and will come in future learning logs.

What I aim to document is how my perception changed over time, and how I used LLMs.

## What I recognised

In the educational stage, LLMs must be used sparingly:

- LLMs made it too easy to do hard things. And when learning, you have to put in the time. When we learned math, we started with mental sums before we moved to calculators to expedite the work. When learning algorithms, we must know how to do it by hand before we rely on LLMs to complete it for us. The fantastic outsourcing of that cognitive load made it especially potent to learners seeking to complete their homework. I realised if I had submitted that algorithm question verbatim, I would have understood how to derive the answer from hints, but it would not guarantee that I would be able to solve a similar question in the future without proper consideration for every line that was written. I found it far better to ask my friend for help, tell him what I had done and why my algorithm should have worked. Until this day, I am enormously grateful to him for sitting with me for hours as I walked him through my code and explained my thought process, which proved very effective. Today I realise it as "rubber-ducking", but of course I preferred a human and it kept me accountable. Writing code is a skill that must be derived similar to writing essays. If we do not know how to write, as software engineers we would be exemplars of the [infinite monkey theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_monkey_theorem), which would make those who may benefit from our code slightly impatient. I may be preaching to the choir, but I strongly advocate against the use of LLMs in areas where cognitive workload is **high** in the context of acquiring **new** concepts.
- LLMs however, should be used for menial tasks. Tasks you already know how to do, but takes too much time. When learning something new, I like to search up and search down, meaning I discover what larger framework or topic my target lies in, and search down, by going into the various branches my topic contains. This mental model is incredibly satisfying to construct. I would consider myself a frequent googler, yet steps like these take the most time. An LLM, based on the data it has been trained on, can get you from a beginner to an informed beginner quickly. Learning how to google is a skill, and can be easily refreshed, and it doesn't mean it has to be done all the time. Although this is about acquring new concepts, the context isn't the key.

Still, the ability to execute tasks based on natural language instructions is a leap in computer science and presents various difficulties and challenges moving forward in this dynamic world.

## What's next
- In 2026, social media has polarising views on AI today. As an individual, learning how to navigate very convincing and seemingly popular beliefs must be approached with critical thinking.
